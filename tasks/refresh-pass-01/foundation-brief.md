# Learning to exercise judgment with AI

Working foundation brief for Judgment Lab · September 22, 2026
Draft for educator review. The teaching approach below is a proposal; its effectiveness across settings has not been established.

A teacher reads a stronger paper, a clearer explanation, or a better recommendation produced with AI. The improvement matters. The teacher still needs to know what the learner understands, which decisions they made, and what they can do when the problem changes. Judgment Lab starts with that practical question and helps educators design work that makes the answer observable.

[The Irreducible Officer](../../the-irreducible-officer.md) develops this argument for professional military education. Officers must direct AI-supported strategic work and remain answerable for the recommendation. The same educational question arises when a university student builds an interpretation from sources or a school student explains an observation. The knowledge, responsibilities, and assistance appropriate to each learner differ.

The work may already contain AI contributions before a learner opens an assistant. A summary can select the evidence. An explanation can suggest which question matters. An assignment can arrive with a problem frame already supplied. Educators should help learners identify those inherited choices and decide which they have reason to accept.

AI-assisted performance is real performance. A learner may use AI to compare more alternatives, test an interpretation, or expose a weakness they would otherwise miss. We should teach that capability and examine whether the help improved the work. We also need evidence of what remains available to the learner when the assistance changes or disappears. In a high-school mathematics study, Bastani and colleagues found that tutor design affected this distinction: unrestricted assistance improved supported performance but reduced later unsupported performance; teacher-informed safeguards largely avoided that harm. The study does not establish the effectiveness of our proposed exercises. [Bastani et al.](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2422633122)

Our starting principle is that people remain responsible for authorizing the purpose of AI-supported work and judging whether its contribution is acceptable. AI can propose goals, frames, and criteria. Educators and institutions decide which should govern an activity, and learners take responsibility for choices within their role. A school student can explain why a claim fits the evidence while the teacher remains responsible for the lesson and its conditions.

Those choices require knowledge and practice. Asking a learner to verify an answer accomplishes little if they do not know what evidence would count or how to check it. Teachers need to establish what learners already understand, demonstrate reasoning where necessary, and provide supported practice. The National Research Council's account of learning connects prior knowledge and explicitly taught thinking strategies to subject understanding. EEF guidance likewise recommends modeling and teaching strategies within curriculum content. [How People Learn](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/9853/chapter/3), [EEF](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/metacognition-and-self-regulation)

The decisions learners own can expand as their understanding develops. A teacher might initially supply the question and demonstrate how to evaluate one claim. Students can then justify a choice, compare alternatives, and eventually challenge the question itself. We should observe readiness in the task at hand. Experience in one subject does not settle what support someone needs in another.

This changes how we decide which effort AI should remove. Source selection, drafting, calculation, and synthesis can each be the point of a lesson. An educator should name the capability that the work develops before delegating it. Where AI can extend the learner's work, the activity should give them something useful to direct and evaluate. Learners need occasions to accept sound assistance as well as to correct or refuse it.

Assessment should reveal a few consequential choices. Keep a starting response, a revision tied to evidence, and a brief account of why a contribution was accepted or rejected. Then change something that matters and ask the learner to respond. Depending on the learning objective, the evidence might come through discussion, a written explanation, a demonstration, or a new problem. A fluent account of the previous answer should face a task it cannot resolve by repetition alone.

Educators should rehearse this work themselves. Two colleagues can inspect the same AI-supported product, compare what they notice, and revise the questions they would ask a learner. Save the example, the review criteria, and the reason for the revision so another educator can examine and adapt them. Shared materials should make their assumptions visible.

Begin with one existing assignment. Identify the intended learning, check the foundations it assumes, and decide what students must practice and what AI could help them do better. Choose a small amount of evidence that will let the educator judge both the work and the learner's response when the task changes.
