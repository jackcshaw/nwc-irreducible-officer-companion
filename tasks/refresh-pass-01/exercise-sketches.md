# Three exercises to test the shared argument

Date: 2026-09-22
Status: Proposed design sketches for educator review. These are not complete classroom packets or reports of trials. High school is the confirmed first K–12 setting; history and science are provisional subject choices.

[Foundation brief](foundation-brief.md) · [Comparison with the spine](spine-comparison.md)

The short sample outputs below are assistant-authored, deliberately constructed examples of possible AI contributions. They are not captured model evaluations or student work. Fictional case facts and data are labeled. Real source packets, educator keys, and discipline review remain part of packet development.

## PME: which problem should the cyber strategy solve?

**Purpose.** Practice connecting a strategic recommendation to the purpose it serves, the evidence available, and the risks it creates. This extends the existing [cyber transfer case](../../cases/cyber-group-strategy-transfer-case.md). Start faculty preparation by questioning the essay's own frame and then transfer the method to the case.

**Proposed case.** In a fictional public-service organization, a leadership team wants to reduce disruption to essential services. A briefing records twelve outages: seven attributed to vendor failures, four to internal configuration errors, and one unresolved. A summary describes all twelve as hostile cyber incidents and recommends putting the entire improvement budget into threat detection. The case must eventually include competing options, resource constraints, uncertainty about attribution, and stakeholder consequences. These facts are invented for this exercise and are not an NWC course artifact.

**Readiness and support.** Learners should distinguish an organizational objective from a technical activity and explain a connection among ends, ways, means, and risk. Ask them to identify one desired outcome, a plausible causal link, and evidence missing from the summary. If they cannot, faculty model that distinction on a simpler case before returning here. Technical vocabulary alone does not establish strategic readiness.

**Human framing.** Students identify whose purpose the recommendation should serve, define success, and explain which parts of the inherited summary they will check. They may contest the supplied diagnosis and compare coherent alternative frames. Faculty retain control of case conditions and assessment criteria.

**AI assistance and learner decisions.** Students first record their frame. They then direct an assistant to compare a small set of responses against explicit criteria and challenge the causal assumptions. The assistant must distinguish packet facts from conjectures. A paper variant uses prepared outputs; it practices reliance decisions but does not establish fluency at directing a live assistant.

Two possible contributions to inspect:

> Useful: The incident summary combines different causes. Compare how each proposed intervention would affect service continuity, and identify what additional attribution evidence would change the priority.

> Misleading: Increased detection coverage is the clearest measure of strategic success, so concentrate resources there and treat coverage growth as evidence of improved resilience.

Students can retain useful detection measures while rejecting their substitution for the actual objective. Disagreement with AI is not the scoring criterion. Record one accepted contribution, one challenged contribution if warranted, and the reason for each.

**Independent change and faculty review.** Introduce new evidence that some events first classified as configuration errors may have a common malicious cause. Ask students to update the recommendation and justify the change in priorities. The assessment must permit several defensible answers. Faculty inspect whether students revise their causal account, distinguish uncertainty from established facts, and stand behind the resource choice. Use oral questioning or an equivalent accessible live response; score the reasoning rather than rhetorical confidence.

**Evidence to retain.** A short initial frame, the consequential reliance decisions, the revised recommendation, and the response to changed evidence. Record whether the AI comparison added a relevant option or check; a more polished brief alone does not establish improvement.

**Faculty practice and reuse.** Two faculty members separately diagnose the sample recommendation, compare the flaw they would teach, and rehearse a useful AI challenge themselves. Save the reviewed case, alternative acceptable judgments, questioning criteria, and one revision note. The institutional artifact is a faculty-reviewed exercise with its assumptions visible.

**Spine check.** This keeps the strongest form of the PME argument: students direct assistance toward an owned purpose and remain answerable under changing conditions. A later advanced exercise could add multiple agents after this capability is observable. Packet development still needs realistic options, a subject-matter review, and a faculty-tested key.

## Higher education: how far does this historical evidence support the claim?

**Purpose.** In an introductory history course, practice constructing a bounded interpretation from primary sources with different authors, purposes, and perspectives. Students own the interpretive claim; the instructor supplies the topic and source-selection conditions.

**Proposed task.** Use a course-approved packet about a specific industrial labor dispute. Students examine a management account, a worker or union account, and a contemporary report with a distinguishable perspective. The instructor must select real documents, supply provenance and relevant context, and establish what questions the packet can answer. No historical quotations or events are fabricated in this sketch.

**Readiness and support.** Learners need enough historical context to understand the documents and must be able to separate a document's claim from evidence about what happened. Ask them to identify a statement, its author and purpose, and one limit on using it as evidence. If needed, model sourcing and corroboration on a short course document; provide an annotated example before independent comparison. Reading and source selection remain part of the learning objective.

**Human framing.** Students write a tentative explanation of the dispute and bound its scope. They name a question the packet cannot settle. Before accepting a supplied or AI-produced summary, they check whose account it emphasizes and which evidence it omits.

**AI assistance and learner decisions.** After direct reading, students ask an assistant to propose an alternative interpretation using only the packet and to identify passages that might challenge their initial claim. Every claimed passage must be checked in the source. The instructor can supply reviewed outputs where live access is unsuitable.

Illustrative contributions, not historical findings:

> Useful: Test whether the disagreement concerns different understandings of the agreement itself. Check each author's description before accepting that interpretation.

> Misleading: The management statement establishes why the dispute occurred; the worker account mainly illustrates the reaction to that cause.

The second output assumes an evidentiary hierarchy it has not earned. It can sound orderly while deciding which voice explains events. Students must justify accepting, modifying, or rejecting the interpretation from the documents. The first contribution is also provisional; the packet may not support it.

**Independent change and instructor review.** Release another authenticated document that complicates an initial interpretation. Students annotate how it changes—or fails to change—their claim and explain why. Review factual grounding, treatment of perspective, proportion between claim and evidence, and responsiveness to the new source. A written source annotation and brief conference, when needed, can replace a separate oral examination. State common criteria so writing polish does not substitute for historical reasoning.

**Evidence to retain.** The initial claim, a source-linked revision, a consequential reliance choice, and the independent response to the additional document. The possible AI benefit is a useful competing interpretation or overlooked relationship; measure that separately from the quality of prose.

**Faculty practice and reuse.** The instructor rehearses the source-bounded prompt, checks whether it imports outside facts, and asks a colleague to diagnose the same synthesis. Save the packet provenance, acceptable interpretive alternatives, rubric adjustments, and preparation/review time. Recheck shared examples when the source packet changes.

**Spine check.** Frame ownership becomes responsibility for the scope and basis of an interpretation. It is not command authority. The exercise preserves evidence evaluation, useful AI challenge, and transfer without asking novices to invent a research field. A history educator must choose the dispute and source packet before this can run.

## High school: what can these temperature readings tell us?

**Purpose.** Practice separating an observed difference from a causal explanation and proposing a comparison that tests the explanation. Science is the candidate subject; the educator must match the lesson to the course and students' preparation.

**Proposed materials.** The following fictional observations are exercise data, not measurements from a real school. Readings use the same stated instrument and are taken at the same time, but the sites differ in both surface and shade.

| Site | Surface | Shade | Recorded surface temperature |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | Asphalt | Full sun | 36 °C |
| B | Grass | Tree shade | 28 °C |

Teacher-set question: what can we conclude about shade from this comparison, and what should we measure next? The learning target is the evidence needed for a causal claim, not knowledge of a particular cooling effect.

**Readiness and support.** Ask students to state the measured difference and name what differs between sites. If they cannot read the table, address that first. If they name only shade, model a different comparison in which two features change together. Supply a diagram or explanation frame if needed. Keep those supports distinct from giving students the conclusion for this task.

**Human framing.** The teacher supplies the question and materials. Students own how far the conclusion can go, which explanation they consider plausible, and what comparison they propose. The teacher chooses the AI output and remains responsible for its suitability. No student AI account is required.

**AI assistance and learner decisions.** Students write or explain their initial interpretation before seeing the following constructed examples. The teacher labels them as prepared examples of AI-style assistance, not as live model results.

> Useful: Both shade and surface type differ between the sites. Compare shaded and unshaded areas with the same surface while keeping other measurement conditions as similar as possible.

> Misleading: The tree shade reduced the temperature by 8 °C. The school should expect that reduction wherever it adds trees.

Students identify the valid arithmetic, the unsupported causal step, and the additional unsupported generalization. They can accept the suggested comparison while improving its controls. They revise their explanation and say what the proposed measurement could establish. AI's candidate benefit is helping formulate a better test after students have attempted the reasoning.

**Independent change and teacher review.** Supply a second fictional case with the surface held constant and comparable conditions, but still only one pair of observations. Ask what stronger claim becomes possible and what remains uncertain about repetition, other locations, or times. Students sketch a next comparison or explain it using the available communication supports. A pupil who repeats “AI is wrong” without using the changed conditions has not met the objective.

**Evidence to retain.** The first explanation, the marked unsupported inference, the revised comparison, and the response to the new case. Assess use of evidence and experimental reasoning; do not reward length or require a full prompt log. An explanation can reveal reasoning even though the learner did not choose the original question.

**Teacher practice and reuse.** Before class, the teacher tries an assistant on the fictional data, saves one useful and one misleading or incomplete contribution if produced, and distinguishes actual outputs from deliberately constructed ones. A colleague checks the science and whether the task demands untaught knowledge. Save the reviewed lesson, common misconceptions actually observed, and one adjustment for next time. Do not populate the misconception record with invented student results.

**Spine check.** This preserves purpose, a consequential reliance choice, responsibility appropriate to the learner, and transfer. It tests the developmental extension: explicit teaching precedes a demand for independent judgment where needed. Later middle- and elementary-school versions must reconsider concepts, reading demands, teacher mediation, and the form of explanation; shorter wording alone is not sufficient.

## Comparison: are these the same task with different nouns?

| Design choice | PME | Higher education | High school |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Learner-owned decision | Which strategic problem and resource tradeoff to pursue | Which historical interpretation the sources justify | Which inference the observations permit and how to test it |
| Frame supplied by educator | Scenario and constraints | Topic, source packet, disciplinary task | Question, data, and AI examples |
| Foundation to teach or check | Causal connection between intervention and strategic outcome | Context, sourcing, corroboration | Data reading, variables, comparison |
| Useful AI contribution | Comparison of options and challenge to causal assumptions | Alternative interpretation tied to supplied evidence | Suggestion of a better comparison |
| Main failure exposed | A technical metric displaces the strategic objective | A plausible synthesis privileges a perspective without justification | A numerical difference is treated as a general causal effect |
| Changed task | New causal evidence changes priorities | New document changes the interpretation | Better-controlled observations change the permitted inference |
| Human review | Defensibility of the recommendation and its risks | Warrant for the interpretation | Understanding of evidence and a testable comparison |

They share a method for making choices inspectable. The professional obligations, subject knowledge, framing authority, and evidence of learning differ. Those differences are the central test of the shared brief.

## Before building full packets

Each educator review should answer: does the exercise teach a real course objective; are its prerequisites reasonable; does the proposed support address a likely gap; does AI add something useful; and does the changed task reveal understanding that the finished product alone would miss? Record the strongest disagreement and the time burden. Revise the exercise before describing it as ready for classroom use.
