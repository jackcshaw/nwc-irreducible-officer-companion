# Teacher key and review criteria

> Superseded classroom draft; optional transfer material only. Start with the [interactive essay lab](../../labs/failure-mode-lab/README.md). See [status and limits](README.md).

Teacher-only material. All example responses below are constructed for calibration; none is actual student work. Accept equivalent explanations and defensible alternative test designs.

## S1 — Initial reasoning

The measured difference is 8 °C; asphalt in full sun is warmer. The table measures surface temperature. Surface material and shade differ together; location and other unmeasured conditions may also differ. The readings establish a difference between these two surfaces at that time. They cannot isolate how much of it shade caused or predict the effect of adding trees elsewhere.

A plausible explanation may include shade, surface material, or both. A student does not need to know their physical mechanisms to notice the evidence problem. “We cannot conclude anything” is too strong: the observed difference is available even though its causes are not separated.

A better comparison could use the same type of surface with and without a shade intervention, comparable starting and surrounding conditions, and a consistent measurement method and duration. Repeated trials and changed positions/assignments can help check consistency and location effects. “Keep everything the same” is incomplete unless the student identifies relevant conditions and intentionally changes shade.

Use S1 to plan support, not to award or withhold access to the lesson.

## S2 — Selective reliance and revision

Note A combines valid arithmetic with two unsupported steps. Its first statement reports the observed difference. Its second assigns the entire difference to shade despite the surface difference. Its third predicts an exact effect everywhere from one comparison. Students may accept the first statement while rejecting or revising the others.

Note B offers a useful design improvement. It does not guarantee a causal conclusion by itself: the learner should consider starting conditions, location, measurement consistency, duration, and repetition. Reward a student who improves it for a reason. Do not require every suggested control, advanced statistics, or the word “confounding.”

An adequate revision might say: “The shaded grass was 8 °C cooler than the sunlit asphalt in these readings. Shade might contribute, but material changed too. I would compare matching surfaces with and without added shade over the same period, check their starting temperatures, and repeat in different positions.” This is an example, not prescribed wording.

## S3 — Stronger evidence, bounded conclusion

The final difference is 6 °C. The sunlit tile rose 7 °C and the screened tile rose 1 °C. The team deliberately varied screen shading, used the same surface type and starting temperature, and assigned the screen by coin toss. These features make the trial stronger evidence about the shading intervention than the original two-site observation.

An acceptable conclusion: “The screened tile stayed cooler in this trial. With the materials and starting conditions comparable, the result supports the idea that this shading treatment reduced surface warming under these conditions.” Students need not compute both temperature rises if their final difference and reasoning are correct.

Do not turn “supports” into “proves an exact universal effect.” There is only one tile per condition and one trial. Random assignment reduces systematic selection but does not eliminate chance differences, measurement uncertainty, or unplanned effects of the screen. The experiment tests a screen and surface temperature, not trees, air temperature, comfort, or health.

Question 2's claim is unjustified: it generalizes to every school and tree, treats the observed difference as an exact constant, changes screen shade to trees, and changes surface temperature to air temperature. A student who identifies two of these with clear reasoning has strong evidence of understanding; require neither a memorized list nor every objection.

Useful next steps include repeat trials with reassignment of shading/positions, more matched pairs, checking sensors, or testing another surface or time while tracking conditions. An extension to trees or air temperature needs those variables actually measured. “More data” needs enough detail to show how it would address a limitation.

## S4 — New context

The stirred, cooler-water cup reaches the stated endpoint 25 seconds earlier. Stirring and water temperature differ together, so this comparison cannot assign the full difference to stirring or establish an “always” effect. Students do not need prior knowledge of which condition normally increases dissolution speed.

A better test holds water temperature, volume, sugar amount and grain size, cup type, and endpoint method comparable; changes stirring; specifies a consistent stirring procedure; and repeats. Students may suggest another sensible design if it separates the explanations and measures the relevant outcome. Require enough detail to make the comparison interpretable, not every listed control.

Examples that challenge the summary include a matched comparison with a different time difference, no detectable difference, or a reversal; each requires a credible measurement/design check. A student who says only “the data are fake” has not answered the conditional reasoning task. The fictional status is explicit so the student can reason from the stipulated observations without treating them as empirical findings.

## Formative review profile

Score each criterion 0, 1, or 2 if useful. Keep the profile; do not convert its sum into a validated mastery cutoff. Use S2 for criterion C, S2's plan for B, and S3 for A and D. S1 remains the initial response, and S4 is recorded separately as later evidence. Pair-supported work on S2 is not independent-performance evidence.

| Criterion | 0 — not yet shown | 1 — partial | 2 — shown in this task |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A. Observation and claim scope | Misreads the outcome or asserts a universal/air-temperature effect without warrant. | Reads the difference correctly but gives a vague limit or explanation. | States an accurate observation and a bounded conclusion tied to the measurement/design. |
| B. Investigation design | Repeats an uninterpretable comparison or changes several relevant features without accounting for them. | Improves one feature but leaves measurement or repetition unclear. | Changes the intended factor, identifies relevant comparable conditions, specifies what to measure, and proposes a meaningful repeat/check. |
| C. Reliance decision | Accepts/rejects a whole note because it sounds right or came from AI. | Identifies a useful/problematic statement but gives a weak reason. | Explains which statement to use or revise with evidence or a design reason. No requirement to dislike AI. |
| D. Response to changed evidence | Repeats the earlier conclusion without considering S3's changed design. | Notices a change but cannot explain its consequence or remaining limit. | Explains why S3 offers stronger evidence and identifies a specific remaining limit or useful next check. |

If a criterion is not elicited or access prevents observation, record **not observed**, not 0. Record arithmetic slips separately when the student's comparison reasoning is otherwise clear.

For S4, record: (a) identifies both differing conditions; (b) bounds the original claim; (c) proposes an interpretable test; (d) names evidence that could change the claim. Note support and interval since the lesson. These observations do not prove durable, domain-general transfer.

## Calibrate with a colleague

Apply A and D only to these constructed S3 responses; B and C are not observed here.

**Response 1:** “The tiles ended 6 °C apart. Screening seems to reduce warming here because they used matching material and starts and deliberately assigned shade. One pair does not establish an exact effect for all places, and they did not measure air. I would repeat with the screen assigned again and check positions.” Suggested A=2, D=2.

**Response 2:** “The difference is 6 °C. It is better because it is a fair test. We need more data.” Suggested A=1, D=1: the answer notices improvement but does not explain which design change matters or what additional check would help. Ask a follow-up before treating terse writing as absent understanding.

**Response 3:** “Both AI notes are unreliable. You can never find out whether shade changes temperature.” Suggested A=0, D=0: it does not use S3's data or changed design. This is a failure to demonstrate reasoning, not a penalty for skepticism. Revisit deliberate comparison and what limited evidence can support.

Treat these scores as calibration suggestions, not ground truth. Discuss disagreements, especially when language fluency or a missing follow-up affects the judgment. Save one clarified criterion and the reason for the change.
