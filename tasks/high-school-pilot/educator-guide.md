# Educator guide

> Superseded classroom draft; optional transfer material only. Start with the [interactive essay lab](../../labs/failure-mode-lab/README.md). See [status and limits](README.md).

## The teaching objective

Students will distinguish an observed difference from an explanation of its cause, identify competing explanations, and propose a comparison that better tests a claim. They will explain which parts of prepared AI assistance they would use, change, or reject. A changed case checks whether their reasoning responds to new conditions.

The task concerns **surface temperature**, not air temperature, comfort, or heat-health risk. The numbers are fictional and illustrate evidence reasoning. No physical experiment is part of this lesson.

## Prepare

1. Read the [student pages](student-handouts.md) and [teacher key](answer-key.md). Keep the key separate from student materials.
2. Print pages 1–3 single-sided for the core lesson. Retain page 4 for a later check. Release one page at a time; page 2 would give away part of page 1's reasoning.
3. Choose access supports: read-aloud, large print, calculator, oral response, diagram, or speech-to-text. Apply them consistently and record conceptual help separately. Do not score spelling, fluency, or length.
4. Try the task yourself. If a colleague is available, independently score the three constructed responses in the key, then compare the criteria used.
5. Choose the one-lesson or two-lesson route. If readiness support takes longer, continue next lesson and preserve the independent check rather than rushing it.

Print guide: the PDF contains student pages only. Student page numbers S1–S4 match the labels in the editable handout. Page 4 is optional for the initial trial but required before reporting any later evidence of retention/application.

## Readiness response

Page 1 supplies the initial evidence. Students should be able to read the table, calculate a difference, and name features that could affect a comparison. Vocabulary such as “confounder” is not required.

| What you observe on S1 | What to do before S2 | What to record |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Trouble reading the table or subtracting | Explain the columns and units; allow a calculator. Ask the student to show the difference again. | Access/number support used. Do not treat arithmetic difficulty as proof of weak causal reasoning. |
| Reads the data but changes several things in a proposed test without noticing | Model a separate example: two toy cars differ in both mass and ramp height. Ask which comparison would help distinguish those explanations. Then return to the temperature task. | Conceptual modeling used; initial answer remains visible. |
| Already identifies the competing explanations | Ask what another measurement could and could not settle. Move to S2 without unnecessary remediation. | Initial evidence of readiness. |

This support is formative teaching. S1 is not a graded gate. A missing prerequisite should lead to instruction rather than exclusion from the activity.

## Run the core lesson

Suggested allocation: 3 minutes introduction, 10 S1, 7 readiness response, 15 S2, 8 discussion, 9 S3, 3 close. Total 55 minutes. Adjust for the class; record actual time.

**Introduce.** Say: “We are deciding what these observations support and what we should check next. The data and AI-style notes are prepared teaching examples. Some parts may be useful, and some may need revision. Explain your reasons.” Do not identify which note is stronger.

**S1 — Initial explanation.** Students work individually with normal access supports. They record an observation, an explanation with uncertainty, and an initial measurement plan. Keep the first attempt; later work goes on S2.

**Readiness response.** Scan the initial work and supply the support above. If the class needs substantial instruction, split the lesson here. Preserve the distinction between initial performance and work after teaching.

**S2 — Use the assistance deliberately.** Students inspect both notes and decide about specific statements, then improve their own plan. Allow pairs to discuss; require each student to record their own decision. Ask “Which part would you keep, and what supports it?” as often as “What would you challenge?”

**Discuss.** Invite different defensible revisions. Distinguish correct subtraction, a causal claim, and a general prediction. Check whether the proposed comparison separates shade from surface material and names what will be measured. A cautious answer should still state what the observations show.

**S3 — Changed evidence.** Collect or put away S1–S2. Distribute S3. Students work individually without the notes, peers, or AI. Keep normal communication and arithmetic accommodations. Do not coach the scientific conclusion. Record any conceptual assistance; that response is supported evidence and should not be labeled independent.

**Close.** Collect S3 before reviewing its key. Name one useful assistance decision and one measurement-design question the class should revisit. The result is evidence for your next teaching decision, not certification of general AI fluency.

## Later check

Use S4 in a later lesson, ideally after some time has passed; record the interval. It changes the topic and restores a comparison with multiple differing conditions. Students use the same access supports, without earlier handouts or conceptual hints. It is a limited application check in a new context. A successful answer alone does not establish broad or lasting transfer.

## Teacher AI rehearsal — optional, before class

This gives the educator practice directing a system. The core paper lesson remains usable without it. Paste only the fictional S1 data and the following prompt into an available assistant:

```text
I am preparing a high-school lesson about evidence and causal claims.
Use only the fictional data I provide. Distinguish an observation from a
possible explanation. Suggest one improved comparison and one question
I could ask students. State what these data cannot establish. Do not
invent measurements, cite nonexistent studies, or assess any student.
```

Compare the response with the teacher key. Ask one follow-up that challenges a hidden assumption or improves the test. Save the prompt, relevant output, and your reason for accepting or changing one contribution. If the system produces no misleading claim, record that; do not relabel an authored example as an observed failure. Actual outputs can replace S2 only after checking their difficulty, factual content, and agreement with the updated key.

## Evidence to keep

If this optional classroom example is later selected for a trial, keep each student's initial response, S2 decision/revision, S3 response, and any S4 response. Record support provided and educator preparation/review time using the [readiness and evidence guide](../readiness-and-evidence-template.md). A classroom pilot sheet has not been developed. Do not require full chat logs. Discuss the result with a colleague and save one reviewed improvement for the next run.
