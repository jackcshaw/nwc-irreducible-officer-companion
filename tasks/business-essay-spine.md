# Judgment at Work: argument spine

Draft for Jack's review, October 27, 2026; revised the same day with his first answers (see Decisions). No essay prose exists yet. This is the business edition of *The Irreducible Officer*, decided in the [business lens plan](../docs/plans/2026-10-26-1800-feat-business-lens-plan.md): it publishes after two business practitioners have run the retention case and the sources below are checked against the papers. The original essay and [claims.md](../claims.md) stay canonical.

## Claim

**Working claim (the original's, carried over):** organizations need people who can direct AI-enabled work toward a purpose they own, decide when the machine deserves weight, and answer for the decision. A finished analysis no longer shows whether they did.

**Sharper thesis for business (proposed):** every company certifies judgment when it approves a recommendation or promotes someone. AI raised the bar for that judgment and hid who clears it, because the deck looks the same either way. Managers have to review for ownership and protect the work that builds it in the people coming up behind them.

The original says that for most knowledge work, judgment hidden inside a finished product "is a convenience," and that it becomes a problem for "an institution whose job is to certify judgment." This edition has to answer that sentence directly: a company is that kind of institution every time it signs a recommendation or decides who is ready for a bigger job.

## Reader

- **Primary:** managers and team leads who review and develop other people's AI-assisted work, and the L&D leads who build their training.
- **Second:** executives who sign AI-shaped recommendations.

After reading, a manager should be able to:

1. review an AI-assisted recommendation by asking for the frame, the reliance decisions, and what would change it;
2. decide which first drafts stay with the people still building judgment;
3. run the retention case once with one analyst and a second reviewer.

## The reframe

Companies are handling AI as a productivity question (how much faster?) and a policy question (which tools, which data?). Both are necessary, and neither tells a manager whose judgment is in the recommendation. The category error is reading the finished deck as evidence of the thinking behind it. The sharpest evidence comes from business itself: in the BCG experiment, consultants who used AI on a task outside its frontier got the answer wrong more often and wrote it up better.

**Jack's point on competition (October 27):** "How do you differentiate competitive intelligence when everybody uses the same intelligence? That becomes a judgment and a fluency of AI use that is hard to capture." When every company can buy the same analysis, the analysis stops being an advantage, and what differentiates is the frame and the skill a team brings to the tools.

*Proposed extension, for Jack to accept or cut:* the property that makes judgment hard to see in a finished deck is the same property that makes it hard for a competitor to copy. The edition's assessment problem and its business case are one argument.

### Counter-evidence the essay must answer

- **AI helps on average.** In the same meta-analysis, human–AI combinations beat humans alone by a wide margin. Aggregated LLM evaluations of business models resemble expert rankings (Doshi et al., *SMJ* 2025). Answer in V: the gains are real, and the losses concentrate in decisions, which is where the manager signs.
- **Sameness can be fixed.** Pooling vendors, chain-of-thought prompting and assigned personas bring AI idea diversity close to human levels (Terwiesch et al., 2026, *POM*). Answer in II and X: convergence is the default, and escaping it takes exactly the fluency Jack describes.
- **Deskilling depends on use.** Learning gains persist when AI is used as a tutor, and apprentices with more AI literacy gained more. Sharing a decision with a machine did not reduce felt responsibility in one experiment (Kirchkamp & Strobel, 2019). Answer in VI and VII: that makes how juniors use AI a design decision for the manager, and responsibility laundering a risk, not a law.

### Corrections

- **Dell'Acqua et al.:** the working paper's abstract says "19 percentage points"; the 2026 *Organization Science* version says "19% less likely." Use percentage points and cite the working paper (84.5% correct without AI against 60% and 70% with it).
- **Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond:** the published *QJE* abstract says 15%; the 14% is from the 2023 working paper.
- **Emanuel, Harrington & Pallais:** now in *QJE* (2026). Check any figure the HE edition took from the 2023 version.
- **Pre-mortem:** Klein (2007) is citable for the practice. Drop the "30% better at identifying reasons" figure; Mitchell, Russo & Pennington (1989) don't support it.
- **Do not cite** Kim, Muhn & Nikolaev's "GPT-4 beats analysts" paper; the authors have withdrawn it over data inconsistencies.

## Objections the essay must answer

1. **Speed is the business.** Competitors who skip the friction will win. *Answer:* most friction should go. The essay protects a narrow set of drafts that build judgment, the frontier result shows the speed gain reversing outside what the system does well, and the careful but slow analyst is the essay's harder case.
2. **This is good management relabeled.** Commander's intent, pre-mortems, decision memos and after-action reviews already exist. *Answer:* agreed, the same way NWC already teaches the method under strategic logic. AI makes it urgent because the finished deck used to carry evidence of the work and no longer does. The contribution is pointing existing practices at AI-enabled work.
3. **Accountability in companies is diffuse.** Committees, matrix reporting and consensus decisions blur who decided. *Answer:* decision rights still name a signer, and where nobody signs, AI makes laundering easier.
4. **AI will soon frame better than your analysts.** *Answer:* keep claim 3's open question open. A system that frames well is still framing toward an objective someone accepted.
5. **The evidence says AI helps novices most, so the worry about juniors is backwards.** *Answer:* the gains are real and measured on output. The Anthropic trial shows output and mastery can come apart, and how the junior uses AI decides which way it goes. That makes it a design question for the manager.
6. **Managers don't have time for this.** *Answer:* that's a real cost. Start with one decision type and one review, and record the actual time.
7. **The essay's conclusion is what an LLM would say.** Models default to recommending differentiation and augmentation, so "differentiate on judgment" can read as trendslop. *Answer:* earn it with the field evidence (Autor, Camuffo, BCG) and let the case show a generic answer failing on a company's own data.
8. **Why would a business reader take an argument written for a war college?** *Answer:* the edition stands on its own. Name the origin once, in the dek, and keep military examples out of the body.

## The essay's case

A fictional regional grocery chain with 40 stores learns that a national discount grocer will open six stores in its markets next year. Leadership asks for a response plan.

- **The AI's answer** is the one every model gives (Romasanta, Thomas & Levina, 2026): don't fight a discounter on price; differentiate on fresh food, local products and service. Its figures check out.
- **Convergence:** every regional chain's AI gives the same advice, so it's also the response the discounter expects.
- **The frame flaw:** the answer describes what regional chains do in general. In most of the affected markets, the chain's loyalty data shows its best customers shop on price and live near the new stores, and its fresh departments rate about average. The generic plan hands those customers to the discounter.
- **Accept after checking:** the AI's price-gap comparison, checked against the chain's own price checks.
- **Changed case:** a market where the chain's pharmacy and fresh counters lead on customer ratings and those shoppers bring in most of the revenue. There, differentiating is right.
- **Why it passes the case tests:** the stock answer is itself the flaw, so no stock phrase catches it; two strong analysts can defend different plans; directing AI helps (sorting the loyalty data by market); something deserves acceptance; and the changed case makes different evidence decisive.
- **Line to try:** "When every chain's AI recommends differentiation, differentiation stops differentiating."

All figures are fictional and must be labeled.

## Avoid

- legal, regulatory or compliance claims;
- productivity numbers beyond what the cited sources report;
- consultant voice ("unlock," "leverage," "transformation"), and "X, not Y" openings;

- any claim of a business result, since no team has run the case.

## Openings

**A. From the reviewer's chair (recommended).** The manager has two retention plans on her desk. One analyst built his by hand from the account data, the cancellation forms and the onboarding records. The other directed three agents, one arguing as the finance lead, one as the head of customer success, one digging into onboarding, and wrote up where they disagreed. Hers reads better, and read cold, you would approve it. Turn in the third paragraph: which analyst understands the business? The plans won't tell you, and companies certify judgment every time they decide.
*Why:* it keeps the original's contrast, puts the reader in the seat they actually hold (the reviewer), and sets up the case Section II uses.

**B. The certification line first.** "Every company certifies judgment. It calls it promotion." Then the two analysts.
*Why not first:* it leads with the stakes, but an aphorism as the first line reads like a LinkedIn hook. It works better as A's turn.

**C. The AI recommendation itself.** Open on the discount-and-annual-plan recommendation and say every number in it is right.
*Why not first:* it's concrete and fast, but it gives away Section II before the reader knows why a manager should care.
