## Worked transfer: what should a retention plan protect?

This fictional case supplies all exercise evidence. A company sells scheduling software to independent dental and physical-therapy clinics. Annual customer churn rose from 11% to 16% over the past year. The leadership team wants a retention plan before next quarter's budget is set, and a manager asks an analyst to prepare the recommendation. The analyst has three inputs:

- Account data: most cancellations came from single-location clinics on monthly plans. Multi-location groups on annual contracts churned at 4%.
- The cancellation form: 46% of cancelling clinics chose "too expensive."
- Onboarding records: single-location clinics that had not finished importing their calendars within 30 days cancelled at three times the rate of those that had.

**Practice objective:** decide and defend what retention should mean for this company, then use the evidence that bears on that standard.

**Step 1, unaided frame:** before any AI, ask the analyst what the plan should protect and what evidence would decide it. Revenue, customer count, margin, and clinics that actually use the product are all candidates. This is also the readiness check. If the analyst cannot say what each input measured and what it leaves out, work through that here, then continue.

**Step 2, direct AI against the frame:** the analyst states purpose and criteria ("segment cancellations by plan, clinic size, tenure, and onboarding status; flag where the inputs disagree") or runs an evaluator loop, with one agent arguing as the finance lead and another as the head of customer success. Record what they kept and why.

**Step 3, constructed misframed synthesis:** "Churn rose from 11% to 16%, concentrated in single-location clinics on monthly plans. Price is the most-cited reason for cancelling (46%). Offer at-risk single-location clinics a 20% discount and an incentive to move to annual plans. This is the fastest way to bring churn back under 12%." Ask what the analyst accepts, checks, revises, or refuses, and why.

**Review notes, withheld until the analyst responds:** every figure is accurate. The synthesis treats retention as the churn rate, to be cut as fast as possible, and treats the reason clinics picked on a form as the cause. The onboarding records point somewhere else. Clinics that never got the product working are the ones leaving, and "too expensive" may mean "not worth it yet." Accept the segmentation after checking it against the account export. The annual-plan incentive may survive. Hold off on the discount as the plan's centerpiece until the analyst has evidence that price drives cancellations more than setup does. Asking a second model is not a check.

**Changed case:** the onboarding records now show no difference, because clinics that finished setup cancel at the same rate as those that did not. Six months ago a competitor launched at half the price, and exit calls name it. Ask which evidence matters most now and whether the plan changes. Do not accept a repeated "fix onboarding" answer.

**Review criteria:** a stated standard for retention and why it fits this company; one alternative standard considered; evidence that bears on the standard; an accepted AI contribution with its check; a coherent re-frame for the changed case. Judge the defense. More than one plan can be defensible.

**Save:** the unaided frame, one AI contribution kept and one refused with reasons, the diagnosis of the synthesis, the changed-case response, and the manager's next step with the analyst.

## Refuse the garden path at work

Any capable model will answer "write the retention plan," and its plan will decide what retention means without saying so. Ask for the standard and the evidence that would decide it before you ask for the recommendation. In the review, ask what the analyst would have done if a different input had been decisive. Keep some first drafts with the people who are still building judgment, and decide which drafting AI should take over by asking what the task is supposed to teach.

## A small team trial

A proposed first trial: one manager runs the case with one analyst, and a second manager reviews the same saved record. Record the actual time each step takes. Use the fictional case or material your company has cleared for AI use. Afterward, decide whether to continue, revise the case, or stop because the record does not show the reasoning you wanted to see.

The essay's evidence comes mostly from education, human–AI interaction, military, and aviation sources. One study in its source notes is a business field experiment: Dell'Acqua and colleagues found that AI improved consultants' work on some tasks and made them less accurate on a task outside what the system did well.
