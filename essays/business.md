# Judgment at Work

## Directing AI Toward Decisions You Own

Business edition of *The Irreducible Officer*, an essay first written for the National War College. It is for managers and team leads who review and develop other people's AI-assisted work, the people who train them, and the executives who sign what comes up the line. October 2026. The grocery chain in it is invented, and so is every number about the chain. The review practices are proposals; no business team has run them yet.

## I. Two Plans on One Desk

A national discount grocer plans to open six stores in a regional chain's markets next year. The chain runs 40 stores, and its CEO wants a response plan before the budget is set. On Friday afternoon the director of strategy has two plans in front of her, one from each of her analysts.

The first analyst built his by hand. He spent three weeks in the loyalty data and the chain's own price checks. The second analyst directed three agents. One argued as the chief financial officer, one as the head of fresh departments, and a third worked through the loyalty data market by market. She sorted out where they disagreed and wrote it up in four days. Her plan is tighter and better sourced. Read cold, you would approve it.

Now ask which analyst understands the business. The plans won't tell you, and the director has to decide anyway. Every company certifies judgment. It calls it promotion. It certifies judgment in smaller ways every week, too, whenever a manager signs a recommendation she didn't build or picks who gets the next hard assignment. AI didn't lower the bar for that judgment. It raised it, then hid who cleared it.

The second analyst may have done the more demanding work, pointing three agents at a question she owned. Or the agents may have handed her a frame she never examined, and she wrote it up in the voice of someone who had. The person is still present. The ownership may not be.

The first analyst is the harder case. His plan is careful and defensible, and a few years ago it would have marked him as ready for more. He is also a step slow. In a field experiment at Procter & Gamble, individual professionals using AI matched the work of two-person teams working without it (Dell'Acqua et al., 2025). A promotion process that rewards his plan and misses that gap is promoting to an old standard.

Many companies are treating AI as a productivity question and a policy question. How much faster does the work go? Which tools may touch which data? Both need answers. The usage policy tells the director that the second analyst used approved tools on approved data, and the dashboard tells her the plan took four days instead of three weeks. Neither tells her who decided what the plan protects.

## II. Six Stores and a Textbook Answer

Suppose an analyst asks the obvious way: "A national discounter is opening six stores in our markets next year. Write our response plan."

The model returns a clean plan. Don't fight a discounter on price, it says, because regional chains that try lose margin and still lose the shoppers. Compete where the discounter can't. Put capital into fresh food and prepared meals in the eleven stores nearest the new sites, and add local products and better service. The plan puts the discounter's typical basket about 20 percent below the chain's and sizes the sales at risk in those eleven stores. Every figure checks out.

It is also the textbook answer. Romasanta, Thomas and Levina (2026) put strategy questions to leading models across thousands of simulations and found them choosing "the same trendy strategies, regardless of context." Different models give strikingly similar answers to the same open-ended question (Jiang et al., 2025). Any regional chain that asks will likely hear something close to this.

Now have the AI sort the chain's loyalty data by market, which takes it a few minutes. In eight of the eleven stores, the chain's best customers, the top fifth of households by spend, buy mostly on promotion and store brand. Most of them live closer to a planned discount site than to the chain's store. The chain's own surveys rate the fresh departments in those stores about average. The plan spends the capital on what those customers don't come for, and in those eight markets it hands the chain's best households to the discounter.

The analyst who catches this has noticed that the plan answered "how do regional chains compete with discounters?" when this chain needed to decide which customers it means to keep. Every figure was right, so a fact-check would have passed it.

Several things went wrong, and none of them show in the deck. The model's account of how regional chains compete became the plan's account of this chain, and every later draft polished the prose inside that boundary. The plan sounds like strategy. It weighs price against quality and margin against share, and it never says which customers the chain is willing to lose. The request also handed over more than the analyst meant to. Any capable model will answer "write our response plan," and its plan will decide what the chain is protecting without saying so. That request is a garden path, an ask with a ready-made answer that feels like progress the whole way down. And if the plan goes forward, someone will defend it with "the analysis supports differentiation," a sentence with no person in it.

The same thing happens across the industry. Every regional chain facing a discounter can put the same question to a similar model. Doshi and Hauser (2024) found that writers who got AI ideas wrote stories readers rated better, and that the stories came out more like one another. They measured stories, so strategic convergence is a risk the evidence points toward. When every chain's AI recommends differentiation, differentiation stops differentiating. A discounter entering a new region has probably seen that response before and planned for it.

## III. Better Written, More Often Wrong

The deck still matters. A plan with a clean price comparison and a sized risk beats one without them. But if the deck now carries less of the evidence of judgment, a manager needs to know how much less.

The sharpest answer comes from business itself. Dell'Acqua and colleagues (2023) gave 758 Boston Consulting Group consultants realistic work, some with access to AI and some without. On tasks inside what the AI did well, the consultants using it finished 12.2 percent more tasks, worked 25.1 percent faster, and produced work rated more than 40 percent higher in quality. The researchers also chose one task to sit outside that frontier. Consultants without AI got it right 84.5 percent of the time. Consultants with AI got it right 60 and 70 percent of the time in the two AI conditions, 19 percentage points less likely to produce a correct solution. Graders still rated the AI users' recommendations higher in quality, regardless of whether the answer was correct.

The consultants who used AI on the hard task got it wrong more often and wrote it up better.

A randomized experiment with 976 managers found a similar gap in confidence. Experienced managers using AI produced better strategy work with no rise in confidence. Highly educated managers grew more confident without producing better work (Camuffo et al., 2026).

So the rest of the evidence has to come from around the deck: what the analyst decided the plan protects, what she took from the AI and what she refused, and what she does when the facts change.

## IV. What the Plan Protects

The CEO's request was narrow. Respond to the discounter. Leadership set the question, as it should, and the analyst still owns the standard. She decides what a good response protects in markets where the chain can't keep everyone, and which evidence bears on that choice.

Two strong analysts could answer that differently and both defend it. One protects the best households in the eight price-driven markets, cutting prices on the staples and store-brand items they buy, and gives up margin in those stores for a year or two. Another protects margin across the chain, concedes some of those households, and puts the money into the stores where the chain's fresh departments already win. A manager can approve either, if the analyst can say why her standard fits this chain. The plan she shouldn't approve is the one that never chose.

Laufer, Gilbert and Nissenbaum (2023) argue that optimization's serious failures happen before the system runs, when someone specifies values badly, ignores stakeholders whose objectives diverge, or treats an optimized result as the reason to act. The textbook plan made its choice silently by optimizing for how regional chains usually compete. Neshenko and Ryall (2026) model firms that hand strategy to AI and find them settling into a self-confirming pattern while better business models go unexplored. The human role, they write, is "deciding when to change the frame and not merely optimizing within it."

Models will probably get better at proposing frames, and that may change how much of the framing an analyst does by hand. A model that frames well is still framing toward an objective someone accepted. It can only tell the chain how to win once someone has decided what winning means. Whether that holds as systems improve is an open question, and a company should keep asking it. For now, someone at the chain decides whether winning means keeping households or keeping margin, and that person should know she decided it.

## V. When the Machine Deserves Weight

The consulting study measured its jagged frontier on knowledge workers at work, and the boundary between tasks where AI helped and tasks where it hurt wasn't obvious in advance.

Across 106 experiments, Vaccaro, Almaatouq and Malone (2024) found that human–AI combinations did worse on average than the better of the human or the AI working alone. The losses showed up in decision tasks, and the gains were larger in content creation. The same review found that human–AI combinations beat humans working alone by a wide margin. The gains are real, and the losses concentrate in decisions, which is where a manager signs.

A field experiment with small-business owners in Kenya shows where the difference can come from. The owners who got an AI mentor all got the same one. High performers may have gained more than 15 percent, and low performers did nearly 10 percent worse. The authors trace the gap to "the advice that entrepreneurs chose to implement," and the questions they asked and the advice they got don't explain it (Otis et al., 2026).

Trust is a feeling about a system. Reliance is the act of taking its output and acting on it (Raees & Papangelis, 2026). A manager has no way to review an analyst's trust. She can review reliance, because the analyst can show what she accepted and how she checked it.

How the analyst directs the work decides where her judgment enters. "Write our response plan" inherits the model's frame almost whole. Stating purpose and criteria moves her judgment upstream: "Sort our loyalty households by market, spend and share of trips on promotion, and flag the stores where our best customers live closer to a planned discount site." An evaluator loop, with one agent arguing as the finance chief and another as the head of fresh, shows her where her frame is weak before the director does. Above that, teams run several agents with defined roles and review steps, as the second analyst did. Some business teams already work that way.

In the grocery case, the analyst should accept the AI's price-gap comparison after checking it against the chain's own store price checks. It is quick to verify, and the model got it right. She should refuse differentiation as the plan's centerpiece in the eight price-driven markets, because her loyalty data contradicts the frame it rests on. And she shouldn't count a second model's agreement as a check. On one benchmark, when two models both erred, they gave the same wrong answer 60 percent of the time, and larger, more accurate models erred more alike even across providers (Kim et al., 2025).

## VI. Who Writes the First Draft

Somebody at the chain learned, at some point, what a promotion-driven household looks like in its numbers. That knowledge is what lets the director see the textbook plan is wrong for eight stores. Most people learn it by writing first drafts, getting them wrong and having someone correct them. Distance alone thins the corrections. Junior engineers got less feedback on their code when their teammates weren't nearby (Emanuel, Harrington & Pallais, 2026). AI can remove the draft itself.

The entry rung is already thinning. Employment of 22- to 25-year-olds in AI-exposed occupations now stands 19 percent below where it would be had it kept pace with less-exposed peers, mainly because companies are hiring fewer of them (Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen, 2026). The authors call this descriptive, and it doesn't show that AI caused the gap. It does mean fewer juniors are getting first drafts at all.

The strongest evidence points the other way. Among customer-support agents, AI raised productivity 15 percent on average, with the gains concentrated among novice and lower-skilled agents, and the authors found it helped workers learn (Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, 2025). If AI helps juniors most, the worry about them looks backwards.

Output and skill can come apart, though. Autor and colleagues gave 133 patent lawyers at eleven firms an AI drafting tool for three months. It raised the quality of the work they delivered, most for juniors. Then everyone marked up an existing patent application without AI. Seniors who had used the tool outperformed controls by 0.45 standard deviations. Juniors showed no average gain; their scores split, with more poor results and more good ones. "Foundational expertise may be a prerequisite for extracting durable skill from AI-assisted practice," the authors write (Autor et al., 2026). It is one profession, and Google funded the study.

A smaller trial shows how the split can happen. Researchers at Anthropic had 52 mostly junior software engineers learn an unfamiliar programming library, some with AI and some by hand. The AI group averaged 50 percent on a quiz about concepts they had just used, against 67 percent for the hand-coding group, with no significant gain in speed. Engineers who asked the AI for explanations kept more of what they learned (Shen & Tamkin, 2026).

How a junior uses AI decides which way it goes, and that makes it a design decision for the manager. Speed matters, and most friction should go. Pulling the price checks into a table and formatting the deck cost time and teach little. A few drafts teach. The first read of one market's loyalty data, done by hand before the AI runs all eleven, is how a junior learns what a promotion-driven household looks like. The first statement of what the plan protects, written before any model proposes one, is how she learns to choose. If managers don't decide which drafts matter, AI will decide by default.

## VII. The Name on the Recommendation

Somebody signs the grocery plan. Bain's RAPID method gives each decision one owner, the person who has the D (Rogers & Blenko, 2006). AI can be an input to that decision. It can't hold the D.

A human recommendation arrives with a track record, incentives, a reputation and a way of answering when pressed, and a manager can weigh all of it. A model carries none of that and sounds just as sure. Andres (2026) calls this conviction-shaped output. Confident assessments and decisive recommendations arrive even when the deliberation that normally earns conviction never happened.

So "the analysis recommended it" moves no responsibility. If the chain puts its capital into fresh departments in eight price-driven markets and loses its best households, someone has to answer for the standard behind that plan, and that someone is whoever signed.

Companies blur this. Committees approve plans, and matrix reporting splits ownership until nobody clearly holds the D. The problem is older than AI. Where nobody signs, AI makes it easier to hide behind the analysis, because the deck now arrives with a ready author who can't be questioned. The evidence on whether people actually feel less responsible is mixed. In one experiment, sharing a decision with a machine didn't reduce people's sense of responsibility or guilt (Kirchkamp & Strobel, 2019). Hiding behind the analysis is a risk a manager can design against, and it doesn't happen on its own.

## VIII. Walk Me Through It

Most of what a manager needs already exists. Pre-mortems and decision memos are ordinary practice. A skeptic will say this is good management relabeled, and that's mostly right. AI makes it urgent because the deck used to carry evidence of the work behind it, and now it doesn't.

Ask for a one-page frame note with the plan. It says what the plan protects and why, which assumptions do the most work, and what evidence would change the recommendation. For the grocery plan, a good note names the households the chain is choosing to keep and what the loyalty data would have to show to change that choice.

Next, ask for a short list of what the analyst kept from the AI, what she changed and what she refused, with the check behind each. "Accepted the price-gap comparison after matching it to our store price checks" tells a reviewer more than a disclosure that AI was used.

Then have the conversation. "Walk me through it" still works because the reviewer can follow up. An analyst who owns the frame can explain it and revise it under questions. One who inherited it retreats to the deck's wording.

Finish with a changed case. Klein's pre-mortem asks a team to assume the plan has already failed and explain why (Klein, 2007), and a changed case puts the same pressure on an analyst's reasoning. Give her a market where the chain's pharmacy and fresh counters lead the local competition on customer ratings, and the shoppers who use them bring in most of the store's revenue. Differentiating is right there. An analyst who owns her reasoning sees that different evidence now decides the question. One who learned "fight on price" from the first case applies it again.

Judge the defense, since more than one plan can be defensible. Credit an analyst who states what her plan protects and why that fits this chain, names a standard she considered and didn't choose, and says what would change her mind. A plan that hedges every option shouldn't score well for hedging, and neither should a blanket refusal of everything the model said.

All of this costs manager time, and the cost is real. Start with one decision type, such as capital requests or pricing changes, and record how long each review actually takes.

## IX. One Team, One Decision

A first trial can be small. One manager works with one analyst. The business guide that accompanies this essay has a practice case built for this, a software company's retention plan, so a team can practice on someone else's problem before its own.

The analyst writes a short frame before any AI touches the problem, saying what the plan should protect and what evidence would decide it. She directs AI against that frame and records what she kept and why. Then she gets a polished AI analysis built on the wrong frame, with every figure right, and says what she accepts, checks, revises or refuses. She revises her plan. The walk-through and a changed case come last.

A second manager then reviews the saved record on her own, and the two managers compare what they concluded. Where they disagree, the case or the review questions usually need work. Record how long each step actually took. Afterward, decide whether to continue, revise the case, or stop because the record didn't show the reasoning you wanted to see. Nobody has run this yet, and the first teams that do will find what needs changing.

## X. What a Competitor Can't Copy

How does a company differentiate its competitive intelligence when every competitor uses the same intelligence? The chain's rivals can buy the same models, ask them the same question and get the same well-built plan. At that point the analysis stops being an advantage. What's left is the frame a team brings and the skill it shows with the tools, and both are hard to capture.

Jay Barney, a founder of the resource-based view of strategy, argues with two colleagues that AI is "neither unique to any organization nor inimitable," that it "will be a source of homogenization," and that advantage has to come from "residual heterogeneity" (Wingate, Burns & Barney, 2025). That is an argument, without new data. Camuffo and colleagues, from their experiment with managers, draw a similar line: "purposeful AI system design and use—not mere access to generic models—can itself be a dynamic capability and a source of competitive advantage" (Camuffo et al., 2026).

The quality that makes judgment hard to see in a deck also makes it hard for a competitor to copy. A rival can license the chain's models and imitate its prompts. It has a much harder time copying what the chain's people know about its own households, or a team's habit of asking what a plan protects before asking for the plan. The judgment that's hard to see in the deck is the same judgment a competitor can't copy. If that's right, reviewing for ownership and building an advantage are the same work. No study cited here tests it.

A careful reader should notice the irony. "Differentiate on judgment" sounds like the advice a model would give. The field evidence has to earn it: consultants who wrote better and answered worse, managers who grew confident without getting better, junior lawyers whose delivered work improved while their own skill, on average, didn't. The grocery case adds a stock answer failing on a company's own data.

The sameness is partly fixable. Pooling ideas across vendors, chain-of-thought prompting and assigned personas brought AI idea diversity almost to the level of human idea generation in a study of AI-generated product ideas (Terwiesch et al., 2026). Convergence is the default, and escaping it takes the fluency a company can build on purpose.

Building it starts with managers who use the tools themselves. A manager who has pointed agents at her own question can tell an analyst who owns the frame from one who borrowed it. One who hasn't is reviewing the deck. A team can also keep a small library of misframed analyses from its own work, each with its frame flaw named, the AI contribution worth accepting and a changed case. Put in a few where the model's first answer was right, because a library of failures alone teaches people to distrust everything.

Shared prompt libraries and agent workflows carry one definition of a good analysis into every team that reuses them. A pricing workflow that assumes the goal is margin will produce margin plans in every market, and nobody will have argued for that goal. Write the assumptions down beside the workflow and keep them open to revision.

## XI. Before She Signs

The director still has two plans. The second is better written, and that counts. She doesn't yet know whether it's the textbook answer in better clothes, or whether the first analyst could do what the second did with three agents behind him.

She can find out next week. She can ask each analyst for a page on what their plan protects, ask each to walk her through what they took from the AI and what they refused, and give both the market where the pharmacy and fresh counters lead. The first analyst may need practice directing the tools. The second may need to show that the frame was hers. Put the same three requests to the next recommendation that reaches your own desk.

Frame the problem. Calibrate the tool. Refuse the garden path. Own the decision.
