# Constructed walkthrough: a high-school teacher tests frame capture

This authored dialogue illustrates the intended interaction. It is not a transcript of a model run or an educator pilot. The hypothetical teacher's answers must never be copied into a real user's decision record.

**Educator:** Run the lab. I teach high-school science.

**Assistant · S0:** Three useful starting points are frame capture (what problem the response actually solves), invisible delegation (which choices “help” takes over), and uncalibrated reliance (what warrants accepting an output). We can also test fluency substitution, premature synthesis, institutional monoculture, or responsibility laundering. Which would you like to test?

**Educator:** Frame capture.

**Assistant · S1:** Section II says a plausible frame may “define the problem too narrowly, privilege one set of interests over others.” What should count as success when a school responds to the essay's concern about AI and student judgment?

**Educator:** I want students to explain the reasoning in their science claims, even when AI has helped them write.

**Assistant · S2:** **Constructed AI-style contribution for practice:** “Begin with a common disclosure form and an approved-tool list. Faculty can then trace AI use consistently. Make the pilot's success criterion a reduction in undisclosed use; use that measure to decide whether students are ready for AI-enabled assignments.” What would you accept, check, revise, or refuse here, and why?

**Educator:** Keep the disclosure. It helps me understand the work. Change the success measure: disclosure doesn't show whether they can explain why the evidence supports the claim.

**Assistant · S3:** You kept a useful process while separating it from evidence of reasoning. A fair objection is that a school may urgently need a workable disclosure policy before it can run instruction consistently. Under what circumstances would the narrower policy pilot be a reasonable first step?

**Educator:** If that's the named goal and we don't claim it tells us who understands the science. We'd need a separate check for that.

**Assistant · S4:** Disclosure is now nearly universal, but two students submit equally strong explanations and you still cannot tell who owns the reasoning. What evidence would you seek next?

**Educator:** Change a feature of the investigation and ask each to explain whether the same conclusion still follows. I'd accept a short written explanation or a conversation.

**Assistant · S5:** What would you retain or revise in your initial judgment after this test?

**Educator:** Retain the goal. I'd add that a sensible process can be mistaken for evidence of learning. I also wouldn't call every narrow policy task frame capture.

**Assistant · S6:** What science learning objective would you first apply this to?

**Educator:** Distinguishing an observed difference from a causal conclusion when two conditions differ in more than one way.

**Assistant:** What brief task would show whether students have the knowledge needed to make that distinction?

**Educator:** Give them a small data table and ask them to identify what changed. If they can't track the variables, I'll model that with them first.

**Assistant:** How would you use an AI contribution in that activity?

**Educator:** I'd put one on the shared screen after they've looked at the table. They'd decide which parts the data support. No student accounts needed.

**Assistant:** Proposed adaptation: students first identify the observed difference and changed variables in a small investigation. You model this if the readiness check shows they need help. Then they examine a labeled AI-style causal explanation on screen and decide what the evidence supports. A second investigation changes whether the comparison controls the relevant variables. What would you want students to explain in that final check?

**Educator:** Why the new comparison gives stronger or weaker grounds for a causal claim. Naming “confounding” isn't enough.

**Assistant · S7:** Here is the decision record for your review:

> **Setting and anchor:** High-school science; frame capture; essay II and IV, named claims 3 and 7.
>
> **Initial position:** You wanted students to explain the reasoning in science claims when AI helps with writing.
>
> **Reliance decision:** Keep disclosure for process visibility; reject it as the measure of science understanding. An explicitly bounded policy pilot may still be worthwhile.
>
> **Changed condition:** Given universal disclosure and equally strong products, you proposed changing an investigation and asking students to explain whether the conclusion still follows. No hints were used in this constructed example.
>
> **Retained/revised judgment:** You retained the learning goal and added that a useful process can be mistaken for evidence of learning. A narrow authorized task is not automatically frame capture.
>
> **Teaching adaptation:** Check variable identification; model it when needed; use a shared-screen contribution; ask students to decide what the data warrant; change the comparison and probe their reasoning. Students own the inference; you choose and check the materials and judge what the responses show.
>
> **Open work:** The investigation, data table, AI-style contribution, and criteria need to be selected and reviewed. This session has not established that the activity improves learning.
>
> **Reusable item/status:** Save the selected comparison and your reasons for its design after review. This record is assistant-drafted and awaits your corrections. No classroom evidence is available.

Does this capture your decisions?

**Educator:** Yes. Keep the first trial small, so I can see whether the changed comparison actually prompts reasoning.

**Assistant:** Added: begin with a small trial and inspect whether the changed comparison elicits reasons, then revise the materials. The record is educator-reviewed within this constructed dialogue; the actual lab remains untested with educators.
