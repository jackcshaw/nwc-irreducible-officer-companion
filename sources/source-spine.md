# Source Spine

Use this file when the reader asks for sources, evidence, or deeper research. Keep facts separate from the essay's interpretation.

## Workplace Evidence (Added October 2026)

These sources were gathered for a business edition in progress. The essay does not cite them. Each note names the claim it bears on, using the numbered claims in the claim map, and where its evidence stops. Several cut against the essay, and they are listed as fully as the ones that support it. Where a detail comes from a working paper, an author's summary or press coverage instead of the published text, the note says so.

**The bar went up, and finished work hides who cleared it.**

- Dell'Acqua et al., [The Cybernetic Teammate](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33641) (NBER, 2025). In a field experiment with 776 Procter & Gamble professionals, individuals working with AI matched the performance of two-person teams working without it. Bears on claim 1, the changed performance standard.
- Camuffo, Gambardella, Kazemi & Pandey, [Beyond Black Boxes](https://doi.org/10.1287/stsc.2025.0432) (Strategy Science, 2026). In a randomized experiment with 976 managers, experienced managers using AI improved the quality of their work without growing more confident. Highly educated managers grew more confident without improving. The authors argue that "purposeful AI system design and use—not mere access to generic models" can be a source of competitive advantage. Bears on claims 2 and 4, finished artifacts and appropriate reliance. Limits: one strategy method and a one-shot task.
- The BCG experiment, listed under Jagged Frontier And Uneven AI Capability below, shows the same pattern in consulting: AI users' wrong answers were rated higher in quality.

**Who sets the frame.**

- Obermeyer, Powers, Vogeli & Mullainathan, [Dissecting racial bias in an algorithm used to manage the health of populations](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aax2342) (Science, 2019). A widely used commercial algorithm, which the abstract calls "typical of this industry-wide approach and affecting millions of patients," helped health systems pick patients for extra care by predicting their health care costs. The abstract reports: "At a given risk score, Black patients are considerably sicker than White patients," and remedying the disparity "would increase the percentage of Black patients receiving additional help from 17.7 to 46.5%." The bias arose "because the algorithm predicts health care costs rather than illness," and unequal access to care means less is spent caring for Black patients. Cost looked like an effective proxy "by some measures of predictive accuracy." Science's editor's summary adds: "Reformulating the algorithm so that it no longer uses costs as a proxy for needs eliminates the racial bias in predicting who needs extra care." Bears on claim 3, purpose expressed through frame: the flaw was the choice of what to predict. Limits: a prediction algorithm, with no generative AI involved. The quotes here come from the abstract and the editor's summary. Details from the full text, including the enrollment cutoff, calibration across races and the size of the manufacturer's fix, have not been rechecked against the published version and are left out.
- Lee, Sarkar, Tankelevitch, Drosos, Rintel, Banks & Wilson, [The Impact of Generative AI on Critical Thinking: Self-Reported Reductions in Cognitive Effort and Confidence Effects From a Survey of Knowledge Workers](https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713778) (CHI, 2025). The authors surveyed 319 knowledge workers, who shared 936 first-hand examples of using generative AI at work. Participants reported critical thinking in 555 of the examples, 59.29% (p. 6). In the paper's words, "higher confidence in GenAI is associated with less critical thinking, while higher self-confidence is associated with more critical thinking" (p. 1). One participant called making a passage professional "a simple task" and "just never thought about it, as critical thinking didn't feel relevant" (P275, p. 11). The paper's name for the alternative is stewardship: "translating intentions into queries, steering AI responses, and assessing if the AI response meets their quality standards for work, while retaining accountability for the work" (p. 13). Bears on claim 4, appropriate reliance, and on invisible delegation. Limits: the survey is self-reported and its links are correlations, so "critical thinking" means what participants perceived they did, and each quote is one participant's. The authors are at Microsoft Research and Carnegie Mellon University. Page numbers follow the [copy hosted by Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf); the ACM copy's pages were not checked.

**Junior development.**

- Autor et al., [Does AI Assistance Enhance or Erode Expertise?](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35720) (NBER w35720, 2026). In a pre-registered three-month randomized trial with 133 patent lawyers at eleven firms, AI raised the quality of the work they delivered on benchmark drafting tasks, most for juniors: lawyers given the tool outperformed controls by 0.34 standard deviations at 10 days (p = 0.03) and 0.38 at 90 days (p = 0.01), as the abstract and p. 2 report. At the end, the 91 lawyers who completed the protocol (p. 1) each reviewed and corrected an existing patent application without AI. Those who had used the tool outperformed controls by 0.32 standard deviations overall (p = 0.04; abstract, pp. 2 and 20), but the advantage was concentrated entirely among the senior lawyers (0.45 standard deviations, p = 0.02; abstract and p. 20). Juniors, those with fewer than seven years of experience (p. 23), showed no average gain. Their scores split, with fewer middling scores and more poor and more good ones, which the authors read as AI assistance serving "as a springboard for some juniors and a cushion for others." The authors conclude: "Foundational expertise may be a prerequisite for extracting durable skill from AI-assisted practice." Bears on claim 5, developmental friction. Limits: one profession and a small junior group. Fifteen of the 91 corrected applications showed possible AI use; without them the overall gain falls to 0.25 standard deviations (p = 0.14), below conventional significance, while the senior gain, at 0.39 (p = 0.06), holds at the 10 percent level (pp. 3 and 27). The authors write that their experiment "does not answer why the effects appear positive for some lawyers and negative for others" (p. 2). The tool was custom, and Google conducted the study and funded its direct costs; in the paper's words, "All firms in the experiment have ongoing patent-drafting relationships with Google LLC" (p. 1).
- Shen & Tamkin, [How AI Impacts Skill Formation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.20245) (Anthropic, arXiv 2601.20245, 2026), summarized in Anthropic's [How AI assistance impacts the formation of coding skills](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills). In a randomized experiment, 52 developers learning Trio, "a new asynchronous programming library," had up to 35 minutes for two short coding tasks, then took a quiz. They were recruited online, and most were professional or freelance programmers: by the paper's Table 1, 29 of the 52 had seven or more years of coding experience, 19 had four to six years and 4 had one to three. Anthropic's summary calls them "mostly junior," which the table does not support. The AI group, whose assistant was GPT-4o, scored lower: "a 17% score difference or 2 grade points" on a 27-point quiz (Cohen's d = 0.738, p = 0.01), with no significant speed gain. The summary gives the averages as 50% with AI and 67% by hand. In an exploratory breakdown the authors did not pre-register, the largest gap was on debugging questions. Among AI users, small groups with different usage patterns scored far apart. The high scorers were Generation-Then-Comprehension (two people, 86%, 24 minutes on average), who had the AI write the code and then asked it follow-up questions to understand it; Hybrid Code-Explanation (three people, 68%), who asked for explanations along with generated code; and Conceptual Inquiry (seven people, 65%, 22 minutes), who asked only conceptual questions and resolved their own errors. The low scorers were AI Delegation (four people, 39%), who "wholly relied on AI to write code and complete the task" and "completed the task the fastest," in 19.5 minutes on average; Progressive AI Reliance (35%); and Iterative AI Debugging (four people, 24%), who "relied on AI to debug or verify their code." Bears on claim 5. Limits: about an hour of work with the quiz straight after, usage groups of two to seven people, and, as the authors note, no job incentive to learn the library.
- Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen, "Canaries in the Coal Mine?" (Stanford Digital Economy Lab, revised August 2026). Employment of 22- to 25-year-olds in AI-exposed occupations stands 19% below the trend of their less-exposed peers, mainly through less hiring; experienced workers show no comparable gap. The authors call this descriptive, not causal. The widely quoted 13% is the August 2025 figure. Bears on claim 5: the entry rung where judgment used to form is thinning.
- Counter-evidence: Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, [Generative AI at Work](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjae044) (QJE, 2025). Across more than 5,000 customer-support agents, AI raised productivity by 15% on average, with the largest gains for less experienced and lower-skilled agents and small quality declines for the most experienced. The authors report that AI assistance "facilitates worker learning." AI can teach as well as replace practice. The 14% figure often quoted comes from the 2023 working paper.

**Reliance and who benefits.**

- Vaccaro, Almaatouq & Malone, [When combinations of humans and AI are useful](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11659167/) (Nature Human Behaviour, 2024). Across 106 experiments, human–AI combinations on average did worse than the better of the human or the AI alone (Hedges' g = −0.23), with losses on decision tasks (g = −0.27) and gains on content creation. Displaying AI explanations or confidence did not significantly help. Counter-evidence in the same review: combinations beat humans working alone by a wide margin (g = 0.64). Bears on claim 4, appropriate reliance.
- Otis, Clarke, Delecourt, Holtz & Koning, [The Uneven Impact of Generative Artificial Intelligence on Entrepreneurial Performance](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2024.06909) (Management Science, 2026). Kenyan entrepreneurs were randomly given access to "a GPT-4-powered AI business assistant." The published abstract reports no detectable average change in revenue or profit. Low performers "did nearly 10% worse because of the AI assistant, whereas high performers may have benefited by over 15%," and the effect for low performers was "over 0.20-standard-deviations lower" than for high performers. The gap came "from the advice that entrepreneurs chose to implement," not from the questions they asked or the advice they got. Bears on claim 4. The revised working paper ([eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/40s41244), PDF dated July 2024) adds detail that has not been checked against the paywalled published text: 640 entrepreneurs, randomly assigned to the assistant over WhatsApp (p. 3) or to business training guides (p. 7); a median entrepreneur who was "26 years old, had been running their business for one year, and held a college degree" (p. 8); "low" and "high" performers who sat below and above the median before the study (p. 9); and, in an analysis the authors call exploratory (p. 3), low performers who were "especially likely to implement 'generic' advice focused on lowering prices and investing in advertising," while high performers found "tailored and specific changes" (p. 4). That version's own effect sizes differ from the published ones, and the February 2024 working paper explained the gap differently: low performers "sought help on more challenging business tasks." Cite the published figures. Limits: the high performers' gain is imprecise ("may have benefited").

**Sameness and competitive advantage.**

- Romasanta, Thomas & Levina, [Researchers Asked LLMs for Strategic Advice. They Got "Trendslop" in Return](https://hbr.org/2026/03/researchers-asked-llms-for-strategic-advice-they-got-trendslop-in-return) (HBR, 2026). The authors' summary on NYU Stern's [research highlights page](https://www.stern.nyu.edu/experience-stern/faculty-research/research-highlights/researchers-asked-llms-strategic-advice-they-got-trendslop-return) reports: "Across thousands of simulations, we saw LLMs almost uniformly select the same trendy strategies, regardless of context." They call "the propensity for AI to opt for buzzy ideas over reasoned solutions" trendslop. The summary names no strategies. The named defaults come only from press coverage of the paywalled article, which reports that the models favored differentiation over commoditization, augmentation over automation and the long term over the short term, with collaboration also mentioned. Practitioner article; its methods are visible only through the authors' summary and that coverage. Bears on claim 3, purpose expressed through frame, and on institutional monoculture. The finding cuts both ways: if "differentiate" is the models' default answer, an argument that recommends it has to earn the recommendation with evidence.
- Kim, Garg, Peng & Garg, [Correlated Errors in Large Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.07962) (ICML, 2025), and Jiang et al., [Artificial Hivemind](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.22954) (NeurIPS, 2025). When two models both get a question wrong, they usually give the same wrong answer, and larger, more accurate models err more alike. Different vendors give strikingly similar open-ended answers. Asking a second model is not an independent check.
- Doshi & Hauser, [Generative AI enhances individual creativity but reduces the collective diversity of novel content](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11244532/) (Science Advances, 2024). AI story ideas made individual stories better rated, especially for less creative writers, and made the stories more similar to one another. It measured stories, not strategies.
- Bottesch, [Towards Generative Monoculture? Insights from a Field Experiment on Style and Content Homogenisation Effects in GenAI-Supported Knowledge Work](https://aisel.aisnet.org/acis2025/290/) (ACIS 2025 Proceedings, 2025). In a field experiment with "99 knowledge workers from a multinational industrial corporation," the abstract reports "significant homogenisation effects" in "style and content when knowledge workers are supported by GenAI (versus when they are not)," across three tasks: knowledge acquisition, knowledge packaging and knowledge creation. It also offers "initial evidence that these effects might be mitigated through advanced prompting and (more) critical handling of GenAI output." Bears on claim 3 and on institutional monoculture: the sameness shows up inside a single firm. Limits: a single-author conference paper from one company, read here in its abstract only, which gives no effect sizes. None of the three tasks was strategy work.
- Wingate, Burns & Barney, [Why AI Will Not Provide Sustainable Competitive Advantage](https://shop.sloanreview.mit.edu/why-ai-will-not-provide-sustainable-competitive-advantage) (MIT Sloan Management Review, 2025), and Barney & Reeves, "AI Won't Give You a New Sustainable Advantage" (HBR, September–October 2024). Jay Barney, who developed the resource-based view of strategy, argues that AI is "neither unique to any organization nor inimitable" and "will be a source of homogenization," so advantage comes from what a firm can do beyond what everyone can access. With Martin Reeves he argues AI amplifies an advantage a firm already has. Argument, not data.
- The BCG experiment, listed under Jagged Frontier And Uneven AI Capability below, found sameness in consulting too: AI users' ideas for a new shoe were better and markedly less varied.
- Counter-evidence: Terwiesch et al., [Artificial intelligence and its impact on creativity and diversity](https://doi.org/10.1177/10591478261474243) (Production and Operations Management, 2026). Better prompting, assigned personas and pooling several vendors brought the diversity of AI-generated ideas close to human levels. Sameness is the default, and escaping it takes deliberate design.

**Who answers for it.**

- Moffatt v. Air Canada, [2024 BCCRT 149](https://www.canlii.org/en/bc/bccrt/doc/2024/2024bccrt149/2024bccrt149.html) (British Columbia Civil Resolution Tribunal, February 14, 2024). Air Canada's website chatbot told a customer they could submit a ticket for a reduced bereavement rate after travelling, within 90 days of the ticket's issue. The customer later learned from the airline's employees that it "did not permit retroactive applications." The tribunal summarized the airline's position: "Air Canada argues it cannot be held liable for information provided by one of its agents, servants, or representatives – including a chatbot." It called the suggestion that the chatbot "is a separate legal entity that is responsible for its own actions" "a remarkable submission" and held: "It makes no difference whether the information comes from a static page or a chatbot" (para. 27). It found that Air Canada "did not take reasonable care to ensure its chatbot was accurate" and ordered it to pay $812.02: $650.88 in damages, $36.14 in pre-judgment interest and $125 in tribunal fees (para. 44). Bears on claim 6, accountability is structural, and on responsibility laundering. Limits: this is one small-claims decision by one provincial tribunal, a record of what happened in one dispute; don't read it as precedent. The decision notes that Air Canada "did not provide any information about the nature of its chatbot," and it concerns a company's liability for its own chatbot, with nothing to say about staff who sign a recommendation.

**Withdrawn.**

- Kim, Muhn & Nikolaev, "Financial Statement Analysis with Large Language Models" (arXiv 2407.17866), often summarized as "GPT-4 beats analysts." The authors withdrew it over inconsistencies in the data and analyses. Don't cite it.

## AI, Learning, And Instructional Design

- Bastani et al., [Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122), provides the empirical caution used in the essay: unscaffolded AI support improved practice performance but reduced later unsupported performance.
- Chow et al., ["AI enhanced my critical thinking": Investigating the paradox of student perceptions and cognitive offloading in GenAI use](https://ojs.aut.ac.nz/pjtel/article/view/246), provides the "epistemic confinement" language used to describe fluency without independent frame ownership.
- Mollick and Mollick, [Assigning AI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.10052), helps frame AI roles in education, including tutor, coach, simulator, teammate, and student.
- Perkins, Roe, and Furze, [The AI Assessment Scale Revisited](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.09029), is useful as a starting point for explicit AI assessment design. For NWC, it needs to be sharpened toward frame ownership, reliance decisions, and final judgment.
- Vendrell and Johnston, [Scaffolding critical thinking with generative AI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.caeai.2026.100572), supports the essay's design logic: preserve productive struggle, sequence AI-free and AI-mediated phases deliberately, and align assessment with reasoning rather than surface fluency.

## Appropriate Reliance And Human Agency

- Raees and Papangelis, [From Trust to Appropriate Reliance](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.23896), supports the distinction between trusting AI and relying on it appropriately.
- Raees et al., [From Explainable to Interactive AI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.15051), supports the move from post-hoc explanation toward user agency, adaptation, and co-design.

## Jagged Frontier And Uneven AI Capability

- Dell'Acqua, Mollick, et al., [Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=64700), provides evidence that AI can improve performance on some knowledge-work tasks and degrade it on others. In the field experiment, 758 BCG consultants, about 7% of the firm's individual-contributor consultants, were split between two sets of tasks (385 on the tasks inside the frontier, 373 on the task outside it) and randomly assigned to work without AI, with GPT-4, or with GPT-4 plus a short prompt-engineering overview. The tasks inside the frontier were 18 product-development questions for a fictional footwear company: ideas for a new shoe, segmenting the market by users, the steps to launch it, a press release and a persuasive memo. Consultants with AI finished 12.2% more of them, 25.1% faster and at more than 40% higher quality. There was a cost in variety: on the first of those tasks, ideas for a new shoe, AI users' ideas were better but showed "a marked reduction in the variability of these ideas compared to those not using AI" (p. 12). On the task built to fall outside the frontier, consultants told a fictional clothing company's CEO which of its three brands to invest in, working from a spreadsheet of financial data and interviews with company insiders. The spreadsheet was designed to look complete, but the deciding details were in the interviews, and the authors designed the task so that GPT-4 would get it wrong. Consultants without AI were right about 84.5% of the time, those with GPT-4 alone 70.6%, and those who also had the overview 60%: in the working paper's words, "19 percentage points less likely to produce correct solutions." Graders still rated the AI users' recommendations higher in quality "regardless of the correctness of their answer." Two of these results are weaker than they sound: the gap between the two AI groups is significant only at the 10% level (p = 0.082), and so is the higher quality rating among wrong answers alone. The working paper (HBS 24-013, 2023) is the source for these figures; the published version (Organization Science, 2026) words the drop as "19% less likely."

## Purpose, Optimization, And Accountability

- Laufer, Gilbert, and Nissenbaum, [Optimization's neglected normative commitments](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17465), supports the claim that optimization embeds normative choices in the decision, objective, and constraints before the system runs.

## AI Diversity And Monoculture Risk

- Deng, Brucks, and Toubia, [Examining and addressing barriers to diversity in LLM-generated ideas](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20408), supports the risk that independent AI outputs may occupy a narrower conceptual distribution than human idea generation.
- Murthy, Ullman, and Hu, [One fish, two fish, but not the whole sea](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.04427), supports the concern that alignment can reduce conceptual diversity in model outputs.

## Apprenticeship, Friction, And Tacit Judgment

- Collins, Brown, and Newman, "Cognitive Apprenticeship: Teaching the Crafts of Reading, Writing, and Mathematics," supports the claim that expert moves must be modeled, coached, scaffolded, articulated, reflected on, and practiced.
- Greg Ceccarelli, [Apprenticeship Was the Point](https://www.meditationsontech.com/p/apprenticeship-was-the-point), sharpens the distinction between wasteful friction and developmental friction.

## Automation Precedent

- Young, Fanjoy, and Suckow, [Impact of glass cockpit flight training on manual flying skills](http://commons.erau.edu/jaaer/vol15/iss2/5/), supports the concern that extensive automation practice can reduce manual skill.
- Sarter and Woods, [Team play with a powerful and independent agent](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1518/001872097778667997), supports the automation-surprise and mode-confusion precedent used in the essay.

## Oral Assessment And Traceability

- Theobold, [Oral exams: A more meaningful assessment of students' understanding](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/26939169.2021.1914527), supports oral defense as a way to probe student understanding under follow-up.

## PME Governance And Faculty Readiness

- Smith, [Educating the AI-ready warfighter](https://www.airuniversity.af.edu/Wild-Blue-Yonder/Articles/Article-Display/Article/4219340/educating-the-ai-ready-warfighter-a-framework-for-ethical-integration-in-air-fo/), is useful for the governance layer: acceptable-use policy, classification tiers, faculty literacy training, and responsible integration. The essay uses it as necessary groundwork, then argues NWC still has to answer the harder teaching and assessment question.
